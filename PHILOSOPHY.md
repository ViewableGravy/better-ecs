# Latticus Gameplay Ideology

## 1. The Vision

**Latticus is a one-world hardcore MMO about building a civilisation from nothing.**

It combines:

* Discovery
* Exploration
* Factory automation
* Character progression
* Real-time combat
* Persistent land ownership
* NPC companions and armies
* Trade and community
* Territorial conflict
* Long-term technological advancement

The player does not simply level up a character or complete a predefined campaign. They enter a persistent world, establish a home, automate production, defend territory, discover new knowledge, build relationships, and gradually transform a primitive settlement into a civilisation.

The central goal of Latticus is:

> **Build a civilisation within a dangerous, persistent world.**

How that civilisation develops is left largely to the player.

A civilisation may become:

* A fortified industrial city
* A trading settlement
* A military power
* A magical research enclave
* A transport network
* A farming community
* A mercenary company
* A coalition of smaller towns
* A remote self-sufficient outpost
* A sprawling automated production empire

Latticus should provide systems, pressure, discovery, and consequences. It should not prescribe one correct way to play.

---

## 2. Core Design Philosophy

Latticus is built around several foundational beliefs.

### 2.1 Progress should be physical

Progress should be visible in the world.

A player should not merely gain higher numbers on a character sheet. Their progress should appear as:

* Roads
* Walls
* Workshops
* Mines
* Farms
* Power networks
* Factories
* Warehouses
* Convoys
* Guard posts
* Towns
* Ports
* Fortifications
* Transport routes

The world itself becomes the player's progression screen.

A developed player should leave behind infrastructure, history, and evidence of their choices.

---

### 2.2 Knowledge should be discovered

The player should not begin with a complete understanding of the technology tree.

They start with little knowledge and learn by interacting with the world.

Discovery may come from:

* Finding new materials
* Studying animals
* Exploring biomes
* Crafting unfamiliar components
* Observing ruins
* Encountering technology
* Learning from other players
* Experimenting with resources
* Unlocking skills
* Recovering lost knowledge
* Meeting factions or specialists

The skill tree provides direction, but it should not reveal the entire future of the game.

The player should regularly encounter moments where they realise:

> “I did not know this was possible.”

Progression should feel like uncovering the structure of the world rather than following a visible checklist from beginning to end.

---

### 2.3 Automation should complement active play

Automation is one of the primary progression paths in Latticus, but automation should not immediately replace participation.

Manual activity should remain faster and more flexible for a substantial part of the game.

Automation should instead provide:

* Consistency
* Offline progress
* Reduced repetition
* Large-scale production
* Resource processing
* Logistics
* Long-term efficiency
* Civilisation-scale output

The intended trade-off is:

> **Active play provides immediate results. Automation provides persistent results.**

A player can actively mine, craft, transport, and fight to outperform a basic automated system in the short term.

However, automation continues operating while the player explores, fights, trades, sleeps, or leaves the game.

This allows two compatible playstyles:

### Active progression

The player directly performs work and gains resources quickly.

### Infrastructure progression

The player spends time designing systems that produce value slowly but continuously.

Neither style should completely invalidate the other.

The strongest civilisations will likely combine both.

---

### 2.4 Time should create value

Latticus should not be designed around constant instant gratification.

Important construction and production may take hours or days.

A personal land claim, for example, requires resources that are processed over several hours before the claim becomes fully established.

This time serves several purposes:

* It makes progress feel substantial
* It allows infrastructure to matter
* It gives players reasons to plan ahead
* It makes logistics meaningful
* It creates vulnerability during expansion
* It rewards persistent preparation
* It allows offline systems to feel valuable

Waiting alone should not be the gameplay.

The player should always have other meaningful activities available while long-running processes continue.

---

## 3. The Beginning of the Game

## 3.1 Arrival

The player enters the world with almost nothing.

They have been sent to conquer and settle the land under the direction of a distant captain or commanding organisation.

The exact motives of this expedition may remain partially unclear at first.

The player is not completely alone.

They arrive with a basic robotic NPC companion assigned to assist them.

This starting companion is:

* A worker
* A helper
* A basic combatant
* A source of early automation
* A bridge into the NPC command system
* A persistent companion within the player's developing civilisation

The robot should not remove the difficulty of the opening.

It should help the player divide their attention.

While the player explores or performs one task, the companion may perform another.

---

## 3.2 Survival and discovery

The opening hours focus on direct interaction with the world.

The player:

* Collects basic materials
* Hunts or avoids animals
* Learns primitive recipes
* Crafts tools
* Explores nearby terrain
* Identifies useful resources
* Searches for a suitable home
* Encounters environmental threats
* Begins understanding the world

The player should not immediately know where the best settlement location is.

Choosing a home should involve meaningful trade-offs such as:

* Nearby minerals
* Fertile land
* Water access
* Defensibility
* Transport access
* Distance from other players
* Nearby hostile creatures
* Access to future technologies
* Proximity to trade routes
* Geographic isolation

The first settlement location should feel like a consequential decision, even if relocation remains possible.

---

## 4. Establishing a Home

## 4.1 The personal claim

Once the player finds a suitable area, they begin creating a personal land claim.

A claim defines land that belongs to the player or their civilisation.

Claiming land requires:

* Resources
* Construction
* Processing time
* Protection
* Commitment

The claim is not instantly secure.

Establishing it takes several hours of background production.

During this period, the player can begin building, but their settlement remains vulnerable.

The claim represents both a gameplay boundary and a simulation boundary.

Within owned land, the player may construct:

* Machines
* Factories
* Conveyors
* Storage systems
* Power systems
* Defensive structures
* Automated production
* Permanent buildings

Outside owned land, construction and automation are deliberately restricted.

This creates a strong distinction between:

* Civilised space
* Wilderness
* Temporary outposts
* Contested territory

---

## 4.2 The first few days

The early settlement should initially feel exposed.

The player constructs primitive buildings, work areas, machines, and storage while gradually gathering enough materials to create walls and proper defences.

After several days of progress, the player should be able to establish a safer perimeter.

This creates a natural early arc:

1. Arrive
2. Explore
3. Choose a home
4. Establish a claim
5. Begin primitive production
6. Survive the vulnerable settlement period
7. Construct walls
8. Create a defensible home

Safety is something the player builds.

It is not granted automatically.

---

## 5. Primitive Industry

The player's first automated systems are slow, inefficient, and dependent on manual labour.

Early industry may include:

* Primitive conveyors
* Hand-powered machines
* Animal-powered mechanisms
* Basic furnaces
* Simple smelters
* Mechanical presses
* Sorting devices
* Storage networks
* Manual loading and unloading
* Basic crafting stations

Early automation should require player involvement.

A player may need to:

* Feed a machine
* Move materials
* Replace fuel
* Repair equipment
* Operate a mechanism
* Transport outputs
* Reconfigure production
* Deal with bottlenecks

The point of early automation is not total independence.

It is the beginning of industrial thinking.

The player starts moving from:

> “How do I craft this item?”

to:

> “How do I create a system that keeps producing this item?”

That shift is one of the central experiences of Latticus.

---

## 6. Simulation Boundaries and Logistics

Owned land functions as an isolated deterministic simulation.

The player can construct traditional automated systems only within claimed land.

The simulation interacts with the wider world through controlled input and output points.

These may include:

* Loading docks
* Stockpile interfaces
* Gates
* Warehouses
* Delivery zones
* Train or wagon terminals
* Resource intake points
* Export slots

This boundary is important both technically and philosophically.

It turns the settlement into a machine whose relationship with the outside world must be deliberately designed.

Resources do not simply appear inside the factory.

They must enter through physical logistics.

Products do not instantly reach the wider world.

They must be exported and transported.

---

## 7. NPC Labour and External Automation

Traditional machines cannot freely operate across the wilderness.

Instead, automation outside claimed land is performed through NPC-led activity.

This includes:

* Gathering expeditions
* Mining trips
* Hunting parties
* Trade caravans
* Supply convoys
* Patrols
* Escorts
* Military deployments
* Construction support

The player's initial robot companion introduces this concept.

Once the player has built harvesting tools and a cart, they may assign the companion to:

1. Leave the settlement
2. Travel to a resource location
3. Gather or mine materials
4. Load the cart
5. Return to the claim
6. Deposit the materials into a stockpile

This creates an external logistics loop without allowing the wilderness itself to become a giant unattended factory.

The NPC and vehicle act as the automation.

This distinction should remain central:

> **Machines automate owned land. NPCs automate the wider world.**

---

## 8. Building a Workforce

As the civilisation develops, the player gains the ability to craft, recruit, modify, or otherwise obtain additional NPCs.

NPCs should become increasingly specialised.

Roles may include:

* Worker
* Miner
* Harvester
* Builder
* Hauler
* Scout
* Medic
* Tank
* Ranged damage dealer
* Melee fighter
* Machine gunner
* Rocket specialist
* Mage
* Engineer
* Animal handler
* Caravan leader
* Guard
* Siege specialist

Early NPCs should be primitive and limited.

Later NPCs may be:

* More intelligent
* Better equipped
* More specialised
* More autonomous
* More expensive
* More difficult to replace

NPCs should not merely be disposable units.

They form the population and workforce of the player's civilisation.

Their equipment, skills, specialisations, and survival should matter.

---

## 9. Formations and Convoys

NPCs can be grouped into formations.

A formation may be assigned to:

* Escort a cart
* Protect a harvesting party
* Patrol a road
* Guard a settlement
* Defend an outpost
* Follow the player
* Support an attack
* Hold a position
* Retreat under specified conditions

A basic convoy might contain:

* One resource cart
* One worker
* One defensive tank
* One healer
* Several damage dealers

As technology develops, this may evolve into:

* Armoured wagons
* Mechanised convoys
* Mobile artillery
* Automated turrets
* Military vehicles
* Tanks
* Air or naval escorts

The convoy system connects factory production, NPC specialisation, logistics, and combat.

It should be one of the defining bridges between the settlement and the wider world.

---

## 10. Combat Philosophy

Combat in Latticus is real-time, positional, and mechanically expressive.

It should support:

* Melee builds
* Ranged builds
* Tank builds
* Healing builds
* Damage-focused builds
* Support roles
* Hybrid builds
* NPC formations
* Large group battles

Combat follows an action-oriented model inspired by projectile-based games such as Enter the Gungeon.

Attacks should often exist physically in the world.

This includes:

* Arrows with travel time
* Bullets with travel time
* Magical projectiles
* Explosives
* Area attacks
* Charges
* Melee swings
* Moving hazards

Players can respond through:

* Movement
* Positioning
* Dodging
* Rolling
* Blocking
* Taking cover
* Formation control
* Timing
* Target selection

Combat should not be determined entirely by equipment or statistical power.

Equipment matters, but player skill and positioning remain important.

---

## 11. Combat Roles

Latticus adopts familiar MMO combat roles while translating them into active real-time combat.

### Tank

Controls space, absorbs pressure, protects allies, interrupts enemies, and draws attention.

### Healer

Restores allies, prevents damage, supports formations, and manages survival during prolonged fights.

### Damage dealer

Applies focused or area damage through melee, ranged, magical, or mechanical weapons.

### Support

Uses control, buffs, debuffs, engineering, terrain, barriers, or utility abilities.

These roles apply to both players and NPCs.

A player may personally fill one role while commanding NPCs that provide the others.

This lets a solo player experience group composition while still rewarding cooperation between human players.

---

## 12. Weapons and Character Expression

Weapons initially provide simple actions such as:

* Light attack
* Heavy attack
* Defensive action
* Basic utility

Over time, the player unlocks skills that can modify or enhance these actions.

Weapons can also be:

* Crafted
* Enchanted
* Upgraded
* Modified
* Repaired
* Combined with technology
* Combined with magic
* Specialised for particular roles

A central principle is that old equipment should not become automatically meaningless.

A stone-age weapon may remain viable if augmented with later-game technology or magic.

For example, a primitive spear might eventually gain:

* Advanced materials
* Powered impact
* Magical effects
* Electrical discharge
* Target tracking
* Improved reach
* Specialised damage
* Defensive utility

The goal is not perfect numerical equality between every item.

The goal is to preserve identity and experimentation.

Players should be able to carry meaningful equipment histories through the ages rather than discarding every object as soon as the next tier appears.

---

## 13. Hardcore Consequences

Latticus is hardcore because actions create lasting consequences.

Hardcore design includes:

* Meaningful death penalties
* Permanent character loss
* Raiding
* Property loss
* Slow and costly progression
* Vulnerable logistics
* Persistent territorial consequences
* Social reputation
* Political consequences
* Difficult recovery from major defeat

Hardcore should not mean arbitrary punishment.

Loss should result from understandable risks, decisions, conflict, or failures.

The player should usually be able to identify what led to their defeat and what could be done differently.

---

## 14. Injury, Defeat, and Permanent Death

Not every combat defeat results in permanent death.

A defeated character may be incapacitated or forced to retreat.

They may teleport back to their town if they survive the initial defeat state.

However, if the character takes sufficient additional damage and is fully killed, that character is permanently lost.

This creates two stages:

### Defeat

The character is incapacitated, removed from the immediate fight, or returned home.

### Full death

The character is deliberately or catastrophically killed beyond recovery.

Full death permanently ends that character.

This distinction creates room for:

* Mercy
* Capture
* Rescue
* Battlefield recovery
* Political leverage
* Revenge
* Escalation
* Meaningful player decisions

Killing a defeated character should carry greater social and political significance than simply winning a fight.

---

## 15. Inheritance

When a character permanently dies, the player continues as an heir.

The civilisation persists, but the individual does not.

The heir may inherit some combination of:

* Land
* Buildings
* Machines
* Resources
* NPCs
* Political relationships
* Reputation
* Knowledge
* Skills
* Equipment
* Debts
* Enemies
* Responsibilities

Inheritance allows the world and civilisation to continue while preserving the weight of permanent character death.

The exact inheritance penalties may evolve during development, but the principle is:

> **Death ends a character, not the player's entire history.**

A death should be painful, disruptive, and memorable without deleting every reason to continue playing.

The civilisation becomes the player's true persistent identity.

Characters are leaders within that civilisation.

---

## 16. Raiding and Property Loss

Land ownership provides control and relative safety, but not invulnerability.

Settlements may be:

* Raided
* Besieged
* Damaged
* Looted
* Blockaded
* Sabotaged
* Captured
* Abandoned

Defences should meaningfully reduce risk.

Walls, troops, traps, terrain, diplomacy, and preparation should matter.

However, permanent settlements should never become completely disconnected from the dangerous world around them.

Raiding should involve commitment and risk for attackers.

It should not become effortless destruction performed against offline players with no counterplay.

The intended experience is strategic conflict, not casual vandalism.

A successful raid should feel like a serious expedition involving:

* Logistics
* Intelligence
* Equipment
* Time
* Combat forces
* Escape planning
* Political consequences

---

## 17. Persistent Social Consequences

The one-world structure means players repeatedly encounter the results of each other's decisions.

Reputation matters.

Players may become known as:

* Traders
* Builders
* Raiders
* Mercenaries
* Explorers
* Engineers
* Diplomats
* Healers
* Criminals
* Protectors
* Warlords

Actions can affect:

* Trade access
* Alliances
* Protection
* Retaliation
* Prices
* Travel safety
* Recruitment
* Political influence
* Settlement relationships

The game should not require formal roleplay.

Social identity should emerge naturally from persistent behaviour.

A player who repeatedly attacks caravans becomes known as a threat because of what they actually did, not because they selected a “bandit” class.

---

## 18. Resource Outposts

Not every valuable resource exists safely within claimed land.

Players may establish temporary outposts near:

* Mines
* Rare forests
* Ruins
* Magical locations
* Hunting grounds
* Strategic roads
* Ports
* Resource deposits

These outposts are not full automated factory simulations.

They may contain:

* Barricades
* Storage
* Camps
* Troops
* Watchtowers
* Basic processing
* Repair facilities
* Loading areas

NPC workers can gather resources while soldiers defend them.

This creates contested economic territory.

A player may decide whether to:

* Quietly extract resources
* Fortify the location
* Negotiate access
* Share it
* Raid competitors
* Hire protection
* Abandon it when threatened

Outposts turn remote resources into points of conflict and cooperation.

---

## 19. Offline Activity and Notifications

Civilisations continue operating while players are away.

Relevant offline processes may include:

* Crafting
* Smelting
* Farming
* Power generation
* Resource transport
* NPC expeditions
* Construction
* Trade routes
* Outpost activity

Because the world is persistent, the player may receive mobile notifications when important events occur.

Examples include:

* An outpost is under attack
* A convoy is being intercepted
* A wall has been breached
* A long-running project is complete
* An NPC expedition has returned
* Power production has failed
* Critical resources are depleted

Notifications should focus on meaningful decisions.

They should not pressure the player to respond constantly to routine noise.

Offline systems should reward planning, not create permanent anxiety.

---

## 20. Progression Through the Ages

The civilisation begins with primitive tools and mechanical systems.

It gradually advances through technological and magical ages.

A possible progression includes:

1. Survival and hand crafting
2. Primitive settlement
3. Mechanical industry
4. Animal-powered production
5. Early magical systems
6. Complex metallurgy
7. Large-scale logistics
8. Electricity
9. Advanced machinery
10. Mechanised warfare
11. Scientific magical augmentation
12. High-level transport and energy systems

The exact eras may change, but progression should feel transformational.

New eras should change how the player thinks about:

* Production
* Movement
* Combat
* Energy
* Logistics
* Territory
* Exploration

Electricity should not merely make existing machines craft faster.

It should enable different kinds of systems.

Later technologies should expand the design space rather than only increasing output numbers.

---

## 21. Magic and Technology

Magic and technology coexist.

The early game may feel more magical because magic can provide capabilities that primitive engineering cannot yet reproduce.

As civilisation develops, science begins to:

* Measure magic
* Contain it
* Amplify it
* Automate it
* Reduce its cost
* Combine it with machinery

The late game does not replace magic with science.

Instead, science augments magic.

This supports a world where:

* Enchantments exist alongside firearms
* Teleportation requires engineered infrastructure
* Ancient weapons accept technological upgrades
* Magical energy can power machinery
* Mechanical armies use magical support

The relationship between magic and technology should become one of the major themes of the progression system.

---

## 22. Exploration and World Expansion

The world is extremely large.

Players should not immediately have access to its full extent.

New parts of the world become reachable as players develop:

* Better roads
* Boats
* Ships
* Advanced carts
* Vehicles
* Navigation tools
* Survival equipment
* Aircraft or other late-game transport
* Teleportation infrastructure

Exploration should remain relevant throughout the life of the game.

Later eras should reveal:

* New continents
* Islands
* Dangerous regions
* Rare materials
* Lost civilisations
* New creatures
* Strategic locations
* Advanced discoveries

The world should feel larger than any one civilisation.

---

## 23. Travel and Distance

Distance is an important strategic force.

Even in the late game, extremely fast travel should require substantial infrastructure or resources.

Travel creates:

* Local economies
* Regional politics
* Supply chains
* Remote settlements
* Dangerous roads
* Strategic ports
* Valuable transport technology
* Reasons to build multiple outposts

Teleportation exists, but it is not free or unlimited.

Early teleportation is primarily magical and expensive.

Later teleportation combines magic with scientific augmentation, reducing its cost and improving its usefulness.

However, teleportation still requires energy or recharging.

This preserves the importance of geography.

Technology should reduce the difficulty of distance without completely erasing it.

---

## 24. Community and Civilisation

The player begins with a personal claim, but the long-term vision extends beyond individual homesteads.

Players may cooperate to create:

* Villages
* Towns
* Cities
* Nations
* Alliances
* Trade networks
* Shared defence
* Public roads
* Industrial regions
* Ports
* Military coalitions

Community should emerge through practical need.

Players cooperate because specialisation, defence, transport, trade, and shared infrastructure provide real benefits.

A civilisation should require more than a collection of nearby buildings.

It should involve:

* People
* Production
* Territory
* Culture
* Security
* Knowledge
* Logistics
* Relationships

---

## 25. Self-Defined Goals

Latticus does not have one final universal victory condition.

The overarching goal is to build a civilisation, but players define what success means within that framework.

Possible goals include:

* Constructing the largest city
* Discovering distant regions
* Mastering a technology
* Building a trade empire
* Becoming a renowned fighter
* Creating an undefeated convoy
* Controlling a strategic resource
* Building a transport network
* Establishing a peaceful community
* Becoming a feared military power
* Preserving a family lineage
* Creating a famous weapon
* Founding a civilisation that survives for years

The game provides direction through discovery, skills, threats, and progression.

It does not reduce the experience to completing a fixed sequence of quests.

---

## 26. One Persistent World

Latticus is designed around the idea of one shared, persistent world.

The importance of this is not merely technical.

A one-world structure allows:

* Shared history
* Long-lived settlements
* Recognisable players
* Regional politics
* Real trade routes
* Persistent conflicts
* Meaningful exploration
* Community identity
* Historical ruins
* Generational inheritance

A location can become important because of what happened there.

A road can matter because players built and defended it.

A ruined settlement can become part of the world's history.

The world should accumulate stories rather than repeatedly resetting into isolated sessions.

---

## 27. Initial Single-Player Development

The first implementations of Latticus will be single-player.

This is a development strategy, not a change to the long-term design.

The single-player version should validate:

* Movement
* Combat
* NPC behaviour
* Resource gathering
* Construction
* Land claims
* Factory simulation
* Logistics
* Discovery
* Progression
* Offline production
* Outposts
* Civilisation growth

Systems should be designed with the eventual MMO direction in mind, but they should not be burdened by premature networking complexity.

The immediate goal is to build a good game.

The MMO architecture should follow once the core simulation and gameplay loops are proven.

---

## 28. Long-Term Networking Philosophy

The eventual multiplayer architecture separates the game into two related simulation models.

### The wider world

The normal world uses streamed state differences.

This includes:

* Players
* Creatures
* Combat
* Projectiles
* Vehicles
* NPC expeditions
* Environmental changes
* Temporary outposts
* Exploration

Clients receive relevant changes based on nearby activity.

### Factory simulation boundaries

Claimed factory areas operate as deterministic simulations.

When a player approaches or joins an active area, they receive:

* The current simulation state
* The relevant simulation inputs
* The current tick or timeline position

The client then runs the simulation locally in sync with the server.

Clients may slightly slow down or accelerate simulation to remain aligned.

Because the factory is deterministic, normal operation requires synchronising only external input and output rather than transmitting every internal machine movement.

Clients and the server periodically compare hashes.

If a genuine desynchronisation occurs, the client performs a full state resynchronisation.

This architecture supports large and complex factories without requiring every conveyor movement to be continuously streamed over the network.

---

## 29. Design Guardrails

Future features should be judged against the following principles.

### Does it help the player build a civilisation?

A feature should contribute to settlement, knowledge, infrastructure, community, defence, logistics, identity, or long-term progression.

### Does it exist physically in the world?

Prefer buildings, items, routes, NPCs, and visible systems over abstract menu-only progression.

### Does it preserve meaningful distance?

Avoid systems that make geography irrelevant without substantial cost or infrastructure.

### Does it support both active and automated play?

Automation should reward planning without making active participation pointless.

### Does it create meaningful choices?

Progression should involve trade-offs rather than one obviously optimal path.

### Does it respect persistence?

Actions should leave consequences in the world.

### Does it preserve discovery?

Avoid exposing every future system and optimal route from the beginning.

### Does it avoid trivialising old content?

Earlier weapons, regions, resources, and systems should retain some relevance through modification, specialisation, or context.

### Does hardcore risk have counterplay?

Loss may be severe, but it should be understandable and influenced by preparation, skill, diplomacy, or decisions.

### Does it strengthen the relationship between civilisation and wilderness?

Owned land should feel organised and programmable.

The wider world should feel uncertain, dangerous, social, and difficult to control.

---

## 30. What Latticus Is Not

Latticus is not primarily:

* A short-session survival game
* A pure factory optimisation game
* A theme park MMO
* A linear technology ladder
* A combat arena
* A fully idle game
* A settlement builder where the player only watches
* A world where automation removes all danger
* A game where character level is the only meaningful progression
* A game where every player is forced into the same objective

It combines these genres, but the civilisation remains the centre.

Factories support civilisation.

Combat protects or threatens civilisation.

Exploration expands civilisation.

Discovery advances civilisation.

NPCs populate civilisation.

Community gives civilisation meaning.

---

## 31. The Intended Player Journey

The intended journey of a Latticus player is:

1. Enter an unknown world with a basic robotic companion.
2. Gather primitive resources and learn through discovery.
3. Explore the surrounding region.
4. Choose land and begin establishing a personal claim.
5. Build temporary shelter, storage, and crafting systems.
6. Create primitive manual and mechanical production.
7. Survive the vulnerable period before proper walls are complete.
8. Build a safe perimeter and permanent settlement.
9. Use the starting NPC to gather and transport external resources.
10. Develop carts, wagons, stockpiles, and logistics interfaces.
11. Craft additional workers and specialised combat NPCs.
12. Establish regular resource expeditions.
13. Build formations to defend workers and convoys.
14. Develop larger automated production chains.
15. Discover new technologies, magic, materials, and regions.
16. Establish remote resource outposts.
17. Defend those outposts against creatures, bandits, mercenaries, and players.
18. Trade, cooperate, compete, or fight with other civilisations.
19. Progress from mechanical power toward electricity and advanced industry.
20. Combine magic with scientific augmentation.
21. Expand through boats, advanced vehicles, and costly teleportation networks.
22. Endure raids, character deaths, political conflicts, and inherited consequences.
23. Continue through heirs when individual characters are permanently lost.
24. Grow a settlement into a village, town, city, or wider civilisation.
25. Pursue a self-defined legacy within the shared world.

---

## 32. Final Statement

Latticus is about transforming wilderness into civilisation without ever fully removing the danger of the wilderness.

The player begins as a vulnerable individual with a single companion.

They gradually acquire knowledge, land, workers, machines, weapons, allies, enemies, infrastructure, and history.

Automation allows their settlement to continue breathing while they are absent.

Exploration reveals possibilities they did not know existed.

Combat gives territory and logistics real stakes.

Permanent character death gives survival meaning.

Inheritance ensures civilisation outlives the individual.

The world should always feel larger than the player, but the player's actions should leave a permanent mark upon it.

> **The character is temporary. The civilisation is the legacy.**
